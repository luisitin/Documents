## 0. Executive summary

1. **The CAA is still in force** and has not been repealed or amended; it remains the fastest and
   most forgiving green-card route for a Cuban who was *inspected and admitted or paroled*. [VERIFIED
   via casaislaw.com (2026) and USCIS "Green Card for a Cuban Native or Citizen"; statute text is
   BACKGROUND]
2. **CHNV parole is "parole" under INA 212(d)(5)** and therefore satisfies the CAA, and the
   one-year physical-presence period does **not** have to be spent in parole status; USCIS says the
   year "need not follow the parole." [VERIFIED, USCIS page] A CHNV parole that has since expired
   or been terminated does not, under the statute's past-tense wording ("has been ... paroled"),
   undo the fact that the person *was* paroled. [BACKGROUND + practitioner sources; see §2.4 for the
   2025 discretionary-factor caveat]
3. **Release on Form I-220A is not parole** (Matter of Cabrera-Fernández, 28 I&N Dec. 747 (BIA
   2023)); the Eleventh Circuit vacated and remanded two such BIA decisions on 2026-02-23
   (*Labrada-Hechavarria v. U.S. Att'y Gen.*), but did **not** declare I-220A to be parole. A South
   Florida class action and a BIA remand decision (expected late 2026 or early 2027) are pending.
   [VERIFIED via AIC, CLINIC, Prada firm page, CiberCuba]
4. **The filing package today** is Form I-485 (edition 09/18/26, mandatory with no grace period per
   secondary sources; fee $1,440 incl. biometrics, $950 for a child under 14 filing with a parent),
   Form I-693 filed *with* the I-485 and valid only for that one application, optional Form I-765
   category (c)(9) at $260 (the H.R.1 "parole/asylum/TPS" surcharge does **not** apply to (c)(9)),
   and optional Form I-131. [VERIFIED for fees and I-693 policy; edition date VERIFIED only via
   secondary sources]
5. **Rollback rule:** the green-card date is recorded as the *later* of (a) 30 months before the
   I-485 filing date or (b) the applicant's last arrival in the U.S.; non-Cuban spouses/children get
   the same rollback. This also starts the 5-year naturalization clock earlier. [VERIFIED via AFM
   23.11 and the 2016 USCIS policy alert]
6. **Official USCIS national median processing time for CAA I-485s** (completed cases): 3.3 months
   (FY2023) → 4.6 (FY2024) → 7.0 (FY2025) → **11.8 months** (FY2026 through 2026-07-31). [VERIFIED
   via CiberCuba/CubaHeadlines reporting of the USCIS historical-median page; the page itself could
   not be fetched]
7. **Volumes collapsed in 2026:** April–June 2026 (Q3 FY2026) USCIS decided only 586 CAA I-485s,
   approving 139 and denying 447 (23.7 % approval), versus 49,965 approvals in April–June 2024 and
   10,432 in April–June 2025; roughly 342,000–355,000 Cuban-category I-485s were pending in
   mid-2026. [VERIFIED via CiberCuba, CubaHeadlines, VisaVerge, Inmigreat reporting of USCIS data]
8. **Three separate government-wide "freezes" hit Cuban CAA applicants** since 2025: (i) the
   2025-02-14 Davidson memo hold on all benefit requests by CHNV/U4U/other parolees, ended by Judge
   Talwani's 2025-05-28 order (*Doe v. Noem*, D. Mass.) and USCIS's 2025-06-09 implementing memo;

   of status as "extraordinary" discretionary relief (challenged in a lawsuit filed 2026-10-05).
   [VERIFIED]
9. **Vetting changes that matter for Cubans:** social-media-handle collection on I-485/I-131/N-400

## 1. The statute and its elements

### 1.1 What the law is

- **Pub. L. 89-732, 80 Stat. 1161 (Nov. 2, 1966)**, codified as a note to 8 U.S.C. § 1255 (INA
  § 245). [BACKGROUND] It lets the Secretary of Homeland Security (originally the Attorney General),
  *in his or her discretion*, adjust a qualifying Cuban to lawful permanent resident (LPR) status
  "notwithstanding" INA 245(c), i.e. the usual bars for people who worked without authorization or
  fell out of status do not apply. [BACKGROUND]
- The 1976 amendments (Pub. L. 94-571) set the rollback period at **30 months**. [BACKGROUND]
- IIRIRA § 606 (1996) provides that the CAA is repealed only when the President certifies that a
  democratically elected government is in power in Cuba; that has never happened. [BACKGROUND]
- As of 2026 the CAA "has not been repealed or amended." [VERIFIED: casaislaw.com, "Is the Cuban
  Adjustment Act Still Valid? Updated guide for 2026 applicants"]

### 1.2 Elements of eligibility (principal applicant)

| # | Element | What it means in practice | Confidence / source |
|---|---------|---------------------------|---------------------|
| 1 | **Native or citizen of Cuba** | Born in Cuba, or a Cuban citizen born elsewhere. Evidence: expired or unexpired Cuban passport showing birthplace Cuba; Cuban birth certificate issued by the civil registry in Cuba; for those born abroad, a Cuban nationality certificate or citizenship letter. A 2019 USCIS Policy Alert ended acceptance of Cuban *consular certificates* as proof of citizenship. A 2022 practitioner advisory says a birth certificate or consular certificate alone is not sufficient; the USCIS page lists the civil-registry birth certificate as acceptable — the safe practice is passport **plus** birth certificate. | VERIFIED: USCIS "Green Card for a Cuban Native or Citizen"; USCIS Policy Alert 20190813-CAA.pdf; visapro.com (2017) on consular certificates; Immigration Justice practice advisory (2022) |
| 2 | **Inspected and admitted *or* paroled** into the U.S. after January 1, 1959 | *Any* inspection-and-admission (e.g., B-2 visa) or *any* parole under INA 212(d)(5), "regardless of the classification of admission or purpose of parole," satisfies this. CHNV parole, CBP One port-of-entry parole, humanitarian parole and Family Reunification Parole all qualify. Entry without inspection (EWI) and release on Form I-220A do **not** (see §2). | VERIFIED: USCIS page; Policy Manual excerpt quoted by citizenpath.com; Cabrera-Fernández commentary |
| 3 | **Physically present in the U.S. for at least one year** | The statute says "at least one year." The year does **not** have to follow the parole or admission: "if DHS paroles a person who has already been physically present for at least one year, they may apply right after parole." Short trips abroad are generally counted against the year only if they break presence; practitioners advise documenting continuous presence (leases, pay stubs, school and medical records, tax returns). The community's **"one year and one day"** rule is a filing convention, not a statutory term: filing on the day *after* the first anniversary avoids any argument about whether the anniversary day itself counts. | VERIFIED (USCIS page) for the rule; "one year and one day" convention is BACKGROUND |
| 4 | **Admissible to the U.S.** (eligible to receive an immigrant visa) | Criminal, security, fraud, health and other grounds under INA 212(a) apply; waivers (I-601) may be available for some. Two grounds matter especially for Cubans: (a) INA 212(a)(3)(D) membership in or affiliation with the Communist Party or another totalitarian party (see §4.5), and (b) health/drug grounds (a 2026 BIA decision reportedly allows denial on the applicant's own admission of drug use at interview, see §4.5). Public charge: CAA applicants are generally treated as exempt from the public-charge ground and do not file Form I-864. | 212(a)(3)(D): VERIFIED (USCIS Policy Manual Vol. 8, Part F, Ch. 3; law-firm summaries). Public-charge exemption: BACKGROUND (8 CFR 212.23(a)) — confirm |
| 5 | **Application filed** (Form I-485) with the applicant physically in the U.S. | No underlying petition, no visa number, no priority date, no sponsor. | BACKGROUND |

### 1.3 Spouse and child derivatives of any nationality

- "The spouse and child of any such alien, **regardless of their citizenship and place of birth**,
  who are residing with such alien in the United States," may also adjust under the CAA if they are
  themselves admissible and were inspected and admitted or paroled. [Statute wording: BACKGROUND;
  the rule that a spouse or child may adjust regardless of nationality as long as the Cuban principal
  qualifies: VERIFIED, CLINIC "Seven Things You Should Know About Cuban Adjustment"]
- Derivatives get the **same 30-month rollback**, "even if that means the individual becomes an LPR
  before the date on which the individual became the Cuban applicant's spouse or child." [VERIFIED:
  AFM 23.11 as hosted by NIWAP]
- VAWA amendments (2000/2005) let abused spouses and children of Cuban principals adjust
  independently; USCIS issued implementing guidance on 2016-07-29. [VERIFIED: NIWAP library and
  LexisNexis note on the 2016 memo]
- The "residing with" requirement is read as residing with the principal in the U.S. at the time of
  adjustment; a derivative whose Cuban spouse already became an LPR under the CAA can still adjust
  (the AFM treats the CAA principal's status as continuing). [BACKGROUND — confirm]

## 2. "Paroled": why CHNV counts and I-220A does not

### 2.1 Two different things called "parole"

| Term | Legal basis | Document | CAA? |
|------|-------------|----------|------|
| Humanitarian / public-benefit parole (what CHNV, CBP One, FRP and "humanitarian parole" grant) | INA § 212(d)(5)(A) | Form I-94 showing class "parole" (CHNV parolees have an electronic I-94 from CBP with a parole period of up to two years) and, for CHNV, the Advance Travel Authorization and USCIS/CBP parole stamp | **Yes** |
| "Conditional parole" / release on recognizance of a detained applicant for admission | INA § 236(a)(2)(B) | Form I-220A, *Order of Release on Recognizance* | **No** (Cabrera-Fernández) |
| Release of someone detained *on arrival* (expedited removal / § 235(b)) | Only § 212(d)(5) parole is available for release in this posture, per *Matter of Q. Li* (BIA 2025) | Varies | Disputed — this is the hook for the pending litigation |

[VERIFIED: AIC blog on Cabrera-Fernández; marcusdefense.com summaries; CLINIC "What Is Going on at
the BIA?"; CiberCuba 2026-10-20 "Why is the I-220A form not a parole?"]

**Practical consequence for the app:** the only CAA-eligible population the app can model with
confidence is people with a § 212(d)(5) parole record (CHNV, CBP One, FRP, humanitarian parole) or
an admission. I-220A holders should be flagged as "eligibility contested — track the litigation" and
excluded from the timing estimator unless they later obtained parole. [INFERRED]

### 2.3 Why CHNV parole is § 212(d)(5) parole

- The CHNV processes (Venezuela Oct 2022; Cuba, Haiti, Nicaragua Jan 2023) granted "parole under
  section 212(d)(5) of the INA" for up to two years after CBP inspection at an airport. [BACKGROUND
  for the mechanics; the DHS termination notice (Federal Register doc. 2025-05128, public-inspection
  copy) and the AIC analysis "Trump Administration Terminates CHNV Program, Impacting More Than a
  Half-Million Immigrants" are VERIFIED as sources describing the program as parole]
- Because the CHNV parolee was *inspected* at a port of entry and *paroled*, both "inspected ... or
  paroled" prongs are satisfied; this is the cleanest fact pattern for the CAA. [INFERRED]

### 2.4 Does terminated or expired parole still count? (2025–2026)

What is verified:

- The statute's wording is past tense — the person "has been inspected and admitted or paroled" —
  and USCIS's own page says the one-year clock need not run during parole. [VERIFIED USCIS page;
  statute wording BACKGROUND]
- DHS terminated the CHNV parole programs by Federal Register notice published **2025-03-25**;
  parolees "without a lawful basis to remain in the United States following this termination must
  depart before their parole termination date." [VERIFIED: FR doc. 2025-05128; AIC] The termination
  date in the notice was 2025-04-24 [BACKGROUND]; Judge Talwani stayed it on 2025-04-14, but the
  **Supreme Court stayed her order on 2025-05-30**, so the termination took effect. [VERIFIED:
  Welcome.US; Wikipedia "Parole for Cubans, Haitians, Nicaraguans, and Venezuelans"; Refugees
  International] On 2025-09-12 the First Circuit held the early termination was likely lawful.
  [VERIFIED: Justice Action Center tracker]
- Practitioner guides written after the termination state that a Cuban "who was inspected and
  admitted or paroled" can still apply under the CAA "regardless of whether their CHNV parole has
  lapsed." [VERIFIED: caunsel.com guide; drmantonya.com "CHNV Parole Termination: Every Legal Option
  (2026)"; lluislaw.com]
- CEDA reported on 2025-03-28 that "some applications to adjust status for those who entered through
  the humanitarian parole program would be put on hold, so some Cubans with CHNV may be unable to
  apply under the CAA" — this described the February 2025 administrative hold (see §8), not a change
  in eligibility. [VERIFIED: CEDA brief; characterization INFERRED]
- The **2025-08-19 Policy Manual (Volume 1) update** lists "parole history" among the factors USCIS
  may weigh negatively in discretionary adjudications, alongside anti-American ideology, antisemitic
  activity and social-media content, effective immediately for pending and new filings. [VERIFIED:
  Fragomen; Boundless; AIILP; Northeastern OGS summary] What "parole history" means operationally
  for a CHNV parolee adjusting under the CAA has not been spelled out in any source found.
- No source found reports USCIS adopting a rule that a *terminated* parole is retroactively "not a
  parole" for CAA purposes. The 2026 practitioner and community sources treat prior parole as
  sufficient. [VERIFIED absence; conclusion INFERRED]

Bottom line [INFERRED]: eligibility survives the end of parole; what changed in 2025–2026 is
*discretion* (parole history as a negative factor; PM-602-0199's "extraordinary relief" framing),
*vetting* (holds, social media) and *exposure* (a person whose parole has ended and whose I-485 is
pending has no status, only a pending application; see §7).

### 3.3 The rollback ("record of admission") rule

- **Rule:** the applicant's LPR date is recorded as the **later** of (a) **30 months before the
  I-485 filing date** or (b) the **date of the applicant's last arrival** in the United States.
  [VERIFIED: AFM 23.11 (NIWAP copy) and USCIS Policy Alert "Effective Date of Lawful Permanent
  Residence" 2016-07-27 (20160727-EffectiveLPRDate.pdf); CLINIC CAA FAQ (March 2022)]
- **Limit:** the rollback can never predate the last entry (AFM example: a Cuban admitted as a
  visitor in 1990 who filed in 1992 could not get the full 30 months). [VERIFIED: AFM]
- **Derivatives:** non-Cuban spouses and children receive the same rollback, even to a date before
  the marriage or birth relationship existed. [VERIFIED: AFM]
- **Why it matters:** the 5-year residence requirement for naturalization (INA 316) runs from the
  rolled-back date, so a Cuban who files promptly after one year can file the N-400 roughly 3.5–4
  years after arrival (N-400 may be filed 90 days before the 5-year anniversary). [Naturalization
  effect VERIFIED in secondary sources; 90-day early-filing rule BACKGROUND]
- **Worked example (hypothetical, like the project owner's cousin):** arrived by CHNV parole on
  2024-02-15, filed the I-485 on 2025-04-01. 30 months before filing = 2022-10-01; last arrival =
  2024-02-15 → **LPR date = 2024-02-15**. N-400 eligibility ≈ 2029-02-15 (early filing window opens
  ≈ 2028-11-17). The rollback is applied at approval, so a long pendency does not reduce it.
  [INFERRED from the verified rule]

### 4.2 What the interview asks (CAA cases)

No official question list exists. Sources describe the following areas: [VERIFIED via Cuba en Miami
(attorney Claudia Cañizares), Cubafull, D-Cuba; BACKGROUND for the standard I-485 review]

- Identity, Cuban nationality and the documents proving it.
- The manner of entry: port, date, parole document, I-94; for CHNV, the sponsor and travel
  authorization.
- Physical presence for one year and any departures.
- A line-by-line review of the I-485 "Part 9" admissibility questions: arrests, drugs, immigration
  violations, misrepresentation, **membership in the Communist Party or other totalitarian party**,
  CDR or mass-organization participation, military/police service in Cuba, persecution of others,

  attorney Armando Olmedo told Univision that Cubans under the CAA are not affected by that memo.

  questions. [VERIFIED: Cubafull; visaverge; attorney quotes are opinions]
- Attorneys report "more detailed" interviews and more scrutiny of records in 2026. [VERIFIED: Cuba
  en Miami]

### 4.4 NBC versus field office: how CAA cases are routed

- The NBC has handled CAA I-485s centrally since at least 2013 (6–8 months quoted then). [VERIFIED]
- Routing logic (general I-485 practice): the NBC pre-processes every case; cases that meet the
  interview-waiver criteria are decided at NBC; the rest are transferred to the field office with
  jurisdiction over the applicant's address. The transfer is visible in case status ("Case Was
  Transferred And A New Office Has Jurisdiction"). [BACKGROUND]
- In 2026, with reported near-universal interviews, the **field office backlog becomes the binding
  constraint**, which is why the applicant's state/city matters for the estimator. South Florida
  offices (Miami, Hialeah, Kendall, Oakland Park) handle the great majority of Cuban cases. One
  undated commercial guide lists Miami I-485 processing at 26 months (80th percentile); another
  gives 21.5–40.5 months — both are for *all* I-485 categories and neither is dated. [VERIFIED
  figures, limited value]

## 10. Implications for the app (design, data pipeline, estimator)

1. **Eligibility gate before any estimate.** Ask: Cuban native/citizen? Manner of entry (CHNV /
   CBP One / other parole / visa admission / I-220A / EWI)? Date of last arrival? Filing date?
   Treat I-220A and EWI as "not currently eligible — litigation pending" and do not produce a
   timing estimate for them.
2. **Regime-aware survival model.** Encode the policy regimes as time-varying covariates:
   (a) 2023–Jan 2025 "fast lane" (median 3–5 months, mostly no interview); (b) 2025-02-14 →
   2025-06-09 parolee hold; (c) 2025-06-09 → 2025-12-02 post-Talwani catch-up; (d) 2025-12-02 →
   2026-06-05/08-24 high-risk-country hold; (e) 2026-05-22 → present discretion memo with
   near-universal interviews and ~24 % approval. A case's expected remaining time is the sum of
   remaining time in the current regime plus transition probabilities to future regimes (court
   rulings). Historical medians alone will badly under-predict.
3. **Show approval probability, not just time.** With a 23.7 % approval rate in Q3 FY2026, "when"
   is less informative than "whether." Display both, with the caveat that denial reasons are not
   published.
4. **Milestone states to collect from users** (self-reported, with dates and field office):
   receipt, biometrics, EAD (c)(9) receipt/approval, "case transferred" notice, interview notice,
   interview held, RFE/NOID issued and responded, approval/card, denial/NTA. Field office and
   state are essential given the shift to in-person interviews; expect a South Florida majority.
5. **Official data to ingest:** USCIS quarterly I-485 by category (CAA line: receipts, approvals,
   denials, pending); USCIS monthly all-forms file (if it carries a CAA line); USCIS historical
   national median page (annual CAA line); the processing-times tool if a CAA category exists. All
   are published with a lag of roughly one quarter, so the app should label data vintage clearly.
6. **Litigation/policy alert feed.** Watch: *Red Eagle Law v. Edlow* (N.D. Cal. 26-cv-04850),
   *Dorcas* appeal (1st Cir.), *Doe v. Noem* (D. Mass. 25-cv-10495), the PM-602-0199 lawsuit filed
   2026-10-05, *Labrada-Hechavarria* remand at the BIA, and the USCIS Policy Memoranda and Policy
   Manual update pages. These events move the regime variable in (2).
7. **Built-in calculators:** (a) earliest filing date = arrival + 1 year (+1 day convention);
   (b) rollback LPR date = max(filing − 30 months, last arrival); (c) naturalization eligibility
   = rollback + 5 years (early filing at − 90 days); (d) fee calculator: I-485 $1,440/$950, I-765
   (c)(9) $260, I-131 ($630, verify), I-693 exam cost, and the H.R.1 surcharge only for (c)(11).
8. **Checklist content** must warn about: I-693 filed with the I-485 and single-use; new I-485
   edition (09/18/26) with no grace period; social-media handles for the last five years; no
   travel without advance parole and the practical risk even with it; Communist Party / CDR /
   military questions and the misrepresentation trap; bringing I-94 and parole evidence, not the
   I-220A, to prove "paroled."
9. **Explain the medians honestly.** The 11.8-month figure is a median of completed cases in a
   period when almost nothing was completed; show pending inventory (~350k) next to quarterly
   completions (586) so users see why their wait exceeds the official number.
10. **Spanish first.** Every surfaced number should link to its CiberCuba/Cuba en Miami coverage
    as well as the USCIS source, because that is where this audience will cross-check it.
